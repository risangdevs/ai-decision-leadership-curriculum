# Phase 4 Source Guide

## Purpose and status

This guide records the principal external foundations for Modules 14–16 and the final capstone. Sources were checked on **25 September 2026**. It is not legal advice and does not replace jurisdiction-, sector-, contract-, labor-, or organization-specific analysis.

AI regulation, standards, technical guidance, and vendor capabilities change quickly. For a real decision:

- use the official source and record the access date;
- confirm effective dates and organizational role;
- distinguish binding law from voluntary guidance or standards;
- check for later amendments, implementing guidance, and sector rules;
- obtain qualified legal, privacy, security, and compliance review where appropriate.

---

# 1. Enterprise AI risk management

## NIST AI Risk Management Framework

- Source: [NIST AI Risk Management Framework](https://www.nist.gov/itl/ai-risk-management-framework)
- Detailed resource center: [NIST Trustworthy and Responsible AI Resource Center](https://airc.nist.gov/)
- Applies to: Modules 14–16, governance framework, risk register, lifecycle gates, and board review.

NIST AI RMF 1.0 provides the **Govern, Map, Measure, and Manage** functions used as a backbone for context-specific risk work. The course operationalizes this through assigned ownership, affected-party analysis, testing, thresholds, residual-risk decisions, monitoring, and lifecycle change.

NIST states that AI RMF 1.0 is being revised. Use the stable concepts, but check the NIST page before claiming conformance to a current version.

## NIST Generative AI Profile

- Source: [NIST AI 600-1: Generative Artificial Intelligence Profile](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ai/NIST.AI.600-1.pdf)
- Applies to: generative-AI risk identification, evaluations, content and data risks, incident disclosure, and supply-chain controls.

The profile expands the AI RMF for risks that are novel to or exacerbated by generative AI. It supports the curriculum’s emphasis on system-level evaluation, data and content provenance, realistic human–AI testing, security, information integrity, monitoring, and documented treatment.

## ISO/IEC 42001:2023

- Source: [ISO/IEC 42001:2023 — AI management systems](https://www.iso.org/standard/42001)
- Applies to: organizational policy, objectives, responsibility, lifecycle processes, assurance, and continuous improvement.

ISO describes 42001 as a management-system standard for establishing, implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an AI management system. Its Plan–Do–Check–Act orientation supports the course’s decision cadence and governance system. The full standard is licensed; do not claim certification or complete alignment based only on the public summary.

## ISO/IEC 23894 and related standards

- Source: [ISO/IEC 23894:2023 — Guidance on risk management](https://www.iso.org/standard/77304.html)
- Source: [ISO/IEC 22989:2022 — AI concepts and terminology](https://www.iso.org/standard/74296.html)
- Applies to: risk language, lifecycle, and integration with enterprise risk management.

Use these as reference points when an organization adopts ISO-aligned terminology or assurance. Select standards based on contractual, regulatory, and certification needs rather than accumulating badges.

# 2. Responsible AI, transparency, and accountability

## OECD AI Principles

- Overview: [OECD AI Principles](https://oecd.ai/en/ai-principles)
- [Transparency and explainability](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/ai-principles/P7)
- [Robustness, security and safety](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/ai-principles/P8)
- [Accountability](https://oecd.ai/en/dashboards/ai-principles/P9)
- Applies to: affected-party communication, challenge, traceability, safety, security, and lifecycle accountability.

The OECD Principles were updated in 2024. They support meaningful disclosure of AI interaction and limitations, information that enables understanding and challenge, robustness across foreseeable use and misuse, traceability, and accountable lifecycle risk management.

The curriculum converts these values into controls: owner, requirement, test, evidence, threshold, escalation, remedy, and review date.

## Singapore Model AI Governance Framework

- Source: [Singapore’s Approach to AI Governance](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/organisations/resources/guidance-by-topic/singapores-approach-to-ai-governance)
- Source: [Model Artificial Intelligence Governance Framework, Second Edition](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/-/media/files/pdpc/pdf-files/resource-for-organisation/ai/sgmodelaigovframework2.pdf)
- Applies to: internal governance, level of human involvement, operations management, and stakeholder communication.

The framework is a practical, sector- and technology-agnostic reference. It informs the course’s human-centric, impact-proportionate approach and its requirement to connect governance to internal owners and operational processes.

## AI Verify

- Source: [AI Verify Foundation](https://aiverifyfoundation.sg/)
- Government context: [Singapore’s Approach to AI Governance](https://www.pdpc.gov.sg/Help-and-Resources/2020/01/Model-AI-Governance-Framework)
- Applies to: technical tests and process checks as evidence for assurance.

The official PDPC page cautions that AI Verify does not set ethical standards or guarantee that a system is safe or free from risk or bias. The same caution applies to every evaluation tool: a test report is evidence for a defined scope, not universal certification.

# 3. Privacy, data governance, and individual rights

## NIST Privacy Framework

- Source: [NIST Privacy Framework](https://www.nist.gov/privacy-framework)
- Implementation material: [Using the Privacy Framework](https://www.nist.gov/privacy-framework/using-privacy-framework-11)
- Applies to: privacy risk, data-processing ecosystem relationships, vendor requirements, lifecycle alignment, and target/current profiles.

The Privacy Framework is a voluntary enterprise-risk tool. It supports the curriculum’s approach to privacy outcomes across internal systems and service providers. Check the NIST site for the status of version 1.1 and do not treat a draft as a final standard.

## UK Information Commissioner’s Office AI guidance

- Source: [ICO — Artificial intelligence](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/)
- Detailed source: [Guidance on AI and data protection](https://ico.org.uk/for-organisations/uk-gdpr-guidance-and-resources/artificial-intelligence/guidance-on-ai-and-data-protection/)
- Applies to: accountability, data protection impact, fairness, transparency, security, minimization, rights, and explainability.

The ICO guidance is useful operational material for organizations subject to UK data-protection law and as a comparative reference elsewhere. Applicability must be determined; it is not a substitute for the law or advice about a specific role and processing activity.

## EU General Data Protection Regulation

- Official text: [Regulation (EU) 2016/679](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj)
- Applies to: lawful processing, purpose limitation, data minimization, accuracy, storage limitation, security, accountability, rights, and automated decision-making where in scope.

Do not convert broad principles into universal one-line rules. Determine territorial scope, controller/processor roles, legal basis, special-category data, rights, transfers, impact-assessment duties, and sector interactions for the actual use.

# 4. AI law and regulatory mapping

## EU AI Act

- Official text: [Regulation (EU) 2024/1689](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/oj)
- Current implementation overview: [European Commission — AI regulatory framework](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai)
- Applies to: prohibited practices, risk classification, providers/deployers, general-purpose AI, high-risk obligations, transparency, logging, human oversight, monitoring, and incident reporting.

As checked on 25 September 2026, the Commission page states that the Act entered into force on 1 August 2024 and became generally applicable on 2 August 2026, with staged exceptions and later dates for specified high-risk categories. The official page also reflects 2026 amendments and implementation material. Verify the current legal text, classification, role, and applicable date before relying on this summary.

The course deliberately teaches a jurisdiction map rather than treating the EU risk categories as a global taxonomy.

## Regulatory mapping rule

For every source of law or regulation, record:

- territory and sector;
- organization’s role;
- system and use-case classification;
- people and data in scope;
- obligation and effective date;
- implementation guidance;
- evidence required;
- owner and counsel confirmation.

# 5. Cybersecurity and adversarial risk

## NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0

- Source: [NIST Cybersecurity Framework](https://www.nist.gov/cyberframework)
- Core document: [NIST CSF 2.0](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=957258)
- Applies to: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover across AI systems and their supply chains.

CSF 2.0 explicitly places Govern at the center and applies to AI environments as part of ICT. It informs the course’s requirements for enterprise ownership, asset and risk identification, protection, monitoring, incident handling, and recovery.

## NIST adversarial machine learning taxonomy

- Source: [NIST AI 100-2 E2025: Adversarial Machine Learning](https://csrc.nist.gov/pubs/ai/100/2/e2025/final)
- Applies to: attack terminology, threat modeling, red-teaming, and mitigation limits.

The report covers attacks and mitigations across predictive and generative AI and emphasizes data, model, lifecycle, and surrounding system context. Use the current edition and its errata; NIST indicates an intent to update as the field evolves.

## OWASP GenAI Security Project

- Source: [OWASP Top 10 for LLM and Generative AI Applications](https://genai.owasp.org/llm-top-10/)
- Applies to: prompt injection, sensitive-information disclosure, improper output handling, supply chain, excessive agency, and related application risks.

Use OWASP as a practical threat and test reference, not proof that a checklist makes a system secure. Validate architecture, identity, data, tools, business logic, monitoring, and recovery in the actual environment.

## MITRE ATLAS

- Source: [MITRE ATLAS](https://atlas.mitre.org/)
- Applies to: threat modeling, realistic attack paths, red-team planning, and incident taxonomy.

ATLAS is a living knowledge base of tactics and techniques involving AI-enabled systems and AI-assisted adversary behavior. Select techniques relevant to the system’s assets, access, lifecycle, and consequence.

# 6. Incident learning

## NIST and incident lifecycle

- Source: [NIST CSF 2.0](https://tsapps.nist.gov/publication/get_pdf.cfm?pub_id=957258)
- Applies to: preparation, detection, response, recovery, and improvement.

The course combines the CSF functions with AI-specific evidence: prompt/instruction version, model and configuration, retrieved sources, tool calls, approval, action, affected cases, and outcome.

## AI Incident Database

- Source: [AI Incident Database](https://incidentdatabase.ai/)
- Applies to: scenario discovery, tabletop injects, and learning from reported harms and near harms.

The database is a public learning resource maintained by the Responsible AI Collaborative. Entries are reports, not automatically verified causal findings. Open the underlying sources and evaluate credibility before using an incident as evidence.

# 7. Decision habits and management-system interpretation

The curriculum’s weekly, monthly, and quarterly cadence is a designed operating practice rather than a requirement copied from one framework. It synthesizes:

- lifecycle and continuous-improvement principles in NIST AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001;
- governance and accountability principles from OECD;
- internal governance and human involvement from Singapore’s framework;
- security governance and continuous functions in NIST CSF 2.0;
- practical evidence disciplines developed in Modules 1–13.

Organizations should change cadence according to decision speed, harm, regulatory reporting, model change, data latency, and incident triggers. A quarterly review is not sufficient for a high-velocity system; a weekly strategy reset can create thrashing.

## Evidence discipline

- Cite law from official legal databases or regulators.
- Cite standards from the issuing body and identify edition.
- Cite vendor capability from current official documentation and verify through testing.
- Cite internal control operation from logs, samples, test results, or audit—not policy text alone.
- Cite realized value from finance/operations records using documented attribution.
- Label professional judgment and ethical trade-offs; do not disguise them as externally proven facts.

## Claims to avoid

- “Compliant with AI law” without scope, role, jurisdiction, and evidence.
- “NIST certified” when using a voluntary framework that provides no such certification.
- “ISO compliant” based only on reading a public summary.
- “Bias-free,” “fully explainable,” “secure,” or “zero hallucination.”
- “Human in the loop” without a tested oversight design.
- “Industry standard” without naming the standard, edition, and applicability.
