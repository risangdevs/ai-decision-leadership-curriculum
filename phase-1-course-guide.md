# Phase 1 Course Guide: AI Decisions and Decision Framing

## Course goal

By the end of Phase 1, the learner should be able to frame an organizational decision clearly, investigate the underlying problem, expose assumptions and stakeholders, and determine whether AI is appropriate for any part of the decision process.

The phase contains:

- [Phase 1 Modules](phase-1-modules[GROUP].md)
- [Phase 1 Artifact Workbook](phase-1-artifact-workbook.md)
- [Phase 1 Source Guide](phase-1-source-guide.md)

The modules form one reasoning sequence:

> **Frame the problem and decision → test causes and assumptions → map stakeholders and authority → evaluate whether AI fits → design oversight → decide and learn**

---

# 1. Required coverage and competencies

## Module 1 — AI & Data-Driven Leadership Decisions

### Topics

- How AI changes organizational decision making
- Decision support versus human judgment
- When AI should be used
- When human judgment should dominate
- Impact of AI on speed, cost, and risk
- Decision suitability for AI
- Automation bias
- Over-trust in AI
- Human oversight

### Competency

The learner can:

- Determine whether AI is appropriate for a decision
- Identify major AI risks
- Evaluate and defend the decision's suitability for AI

## Module 2 — Decision Framing

### Topics

- Symptoms versus problems versus decisions
- Root-cause analysis
- 5 Whys Framework
- Course 4P Decision Framework: Problem, People, Process, Parameters
- Decision statements
- Assumption identification and validation
- Stakeholder mapping
- Actionable decision framing

### Competency

The learner can:

- Identify plausible root causes systematically
- Write clear, actionable decision statements
- Validate high-impact assumptions before proposing solutions

---

# 2. Learning contract

This phase is taught through dialogue and application, not content exposure alone. Use the following cycle repeatedly:

1. Ask what the learner already knows.
2. Introduce concepts gradually.
3. Challenge the learner's thinking.
4. Present a realistic business case.
5. Ask the learner to solve it before revealing a model response.
6. Critique the reasoning, not just the conclusion.
7. Reveal blind spots, missing evidence, and hidden assumptions.
8. Improve the answer and artifacts together.

## Progression rule

Do not progress because the learner agrees with the facilitator or repeats a definition. Progress when the learner can:

- Explain the concept in their own words
- Apply it to a case not used in the explanation
- Defend the reasoning under skeptical follow-up
- Identify what evidence would change the conclusion
- Revise an artifact after critique

When the learner is not yet competent, diagnose the gap, provide a smaller example, and repeat the application cycle.

## Core questions

Ask these throughout both modules:

- Why do you believe this?
- What evidence supports it?
- Could AI be wrong?
- What assumptions exist?
- Who owns this decision?
- What data would change your mind?

Additional challenge questions:

- Is that an observation, interpretation, or causal claim?
- Compared with what alternative?
- Who benefits and who bears the error?
- What happens when the system encounters something new?
- Does a human reviewer have real authority or only nominal responsibility?
- Have you framed the choice so narrowly that your preferred solution becomes inevitable?

# 3. Recommended course sequence

The sequence below assumes six facilitated sessions plus independent artifact work. Timing should adapt to demonstrated understanding.

| Session | Focus | Learner performance | Artifact update |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Diagnose the starting point** | Current mental models; symptoms, problems, decisions, solutions | Classifies mixed statements and exposes assumptions | Start Root Cause Analysis and Assumption Log |
| **2. Investigate causes** | RCA, branching 5 Whys, causal evidence | Develops competing explanations and disconfirming tests | Revise RCA; prioritize assumptions |
| **3. Frame the decision** | 4P, stakeholders, decision statement | Names the owner, alternatives, objectives, evidence, and constraints | Create Decision Statement and Stakeholder Map |
| **4. Evaluate AI fit** | AI roles, suitability, alternatives, speed/cost/risk | Determines whether and where AI adds value | Start AI Decision Suitability Canvas |
| **5. Design authority and controls** | Human judgment, automation bias, errors, oversight | Defines human authority, intervention triggers, and accountability | Revise canvas and stakeholder roles |
| **6. Integrate and defend** | Portfolio implications, stage gates, learning plan | Defends all five artifacts under challenge | Final Phase 1 artifact set |

## Optional application rhythm

Between sessions, the learner should apply the tools to one real organizational decision. At the beginning of the next session, ask:

1. What changed in your frame?
2. Which assumption became weaker or stronger?
3. Which stakeholder perspective altered the decision?
4. Did AI become more or less suitable—and why?

# 4. Evolving outputs

The outputs should evolve together. They are not independent forms completed once.

Use the editable [Phase 1 Artifact Workbook](phase-1-artifact-workbook.md) to maintain all five outputs and their revision history in one place.

## 1. Root Cause Analysis

Purpose: distinguish the observed problem from plausible causal mechanisms and intervention points.

Minimum quality:

- Neutral problem statement
- Multiple causal branches where appropriate
- Evidence for and against important links
- Clear distinction between causes and assumptions
- Identification of actionable intervention points

## 2. Decision Statement

Purpose: state who must choose what, by when, for which outcome, using what evidence, and under which constraints.

Minimum quality:

- Named decision owner
- Genuine alternatives
- Objective separate from the preferred solution
- Timeframe, scope, and constraints
- Decision-relevant evidence
- Neutral wording that permits more than one conclusion

## 3. Stakeholder Map

Purpose: identify authority, expertise, implementation responsibilities, exposure to consequences, and required involvement.

Minimum quality:

- Decision owner and sponsor
- Process performers and action owners
- Evidence providers and independent challengers
- Affected parties, including low-power groups
- Engagement or participation appropriate to role and consequence

## 4. Assumption Log

Purpose: prevent uncertain claims from silently controlling the decision.

Minimum quality:

- Assumption category and evidence status
- Consequence if wrong
- Uncertainty and impact
- Validation method, owner, and review date
- Evidence that the log changes as learning occurs

## 5. AI Decision Suitability Canvas

Purpose: determine whether AI should be used at all and, if so, for which decision point, in which role, and under whose authority.

Minimum quality:

- AI compared with feasible non-AI alternatives
- Specific AI role: observe, analyze, predict, recommend, or automate
- Error consequences and affected parties
- Human judgment boundary
- Review, challenge, override, escalation, and stop triggers
- Named accountability and learning plan

## Change relationships

| If this changes… | Revisit… |
|---|---|
| Root-cause explanation | Decision alternatives, assumptions, and AI suitability |
| Decision owner or objective | Stakeholder map, canvas authority, success measures |
| Affected population | Stakeholders, data fitness, error consequences, oversight |
| Key assumption fails | RCA, decision statement, and proposed solution |
| AI role expands | Human oversight, risk measures, approval stage, and accountability |
| Data source or vendor changes | Assumption log, canvas, monitoring, and portfolio dependencies |

# 5. Using ChatGPT as a thinking partner

ChatGPT should expand and challenge reasoning, not become the decision owner.

## Good uses

- Classify symptoms, causes, assumptions, decisions, and solutions
- Generate competing hypotheses and alternative frames
- Role-play stakeholders or a skeptical executive
- Challenge a decision statement for solution bias
- Suggest disconfirming evidence and assumption tests
- Structure the five evolving artifacts
- Create an unfamiliar practice case
- Conduct one-question-at-a-time oral examination

## Required safeguards

- Verify facts, sources, calculations, and citations
- Mark AI-generated hypotheses as hypotheses
- Do not infer stakeholder views without consulting them
- Do not submit confidential material to an unapproved tool
- Keep human accountability for objectives, trade-offs, and final decisions

## Tutor prompt

> Act as a demanding decision-framing tutor. Ask what I believe before teaching. Introduce one concept at a time, then give me a business case and require my answer. Ask one difficult follow-up at a time. Frequently ask: Why do you believe this? What evidence supports it? Could AI be wrong? What assumptions exist? Who owns this decision? What data would change your mind? Critique my reasoning, reveal blind spots, and require me to revise the relevant artifact. Do not advance until I demonstrate transfer to a new case. Do not make the final organizational decision for me.

# 6. Mastery gates

## Gate A — Problem diagnosis

The learner must be able to:

- Separate symptoms, problems, causes, decisions, and solutions
- Produce more than one plausible causal explanation
- Identify evidence needed to discriminate among explanations
- Avoid stopping at blame or the first plausible cause

## Gate B — Decision framing

The learner must be able to:

- Apply Problem, People, Process, and Parameters
- Map authority and impact, not only interest
- Write a decision statement with genuine alternatives
- Prioritize assumptions by consequence and uncertainty

## Gate C — AI suitability

The learner must be able to:

- Compare AI with simpler alternatives
- Choose a specific and proportionate AI role
- Analyze false positives, false negatives, novelty, reversibility, and scale
- Explain when human judgment must dominate

## Gate D — Governance and learning

The learner must be able to:

- Design meaningful human review rather than a rubber stamp
- Define challenge, override, escalation, and stop authority
- Name accountable owners
- Specify evidence for piloting, scaling, restricting, or retiring the system

Failure at any gate leads to focused practice and artifact revision before advancement.

# 7. End-of-phase oral examination

The examination evaluates reasoning under pressure, not memorization. Conduct it conversationally, with one question at a time. Do not provide hints until the learner has committed to an answer.

## Examination structure

### Part 1 — Artifact defense

The learner presents the five artifacts for one decision in no more than five minutes. The examiner then challenges:

- The problem definition
- At least two causal claims
- The decision owner and alternatives
- One missing or low-power stakeholder
- The highest-priority assumption
- The proposed AI role and non-AI comparator
- The most consequential error
- Human authority and stop conditions

### Part 2 — Unseen case

Use this case or an equivalent unfamiliar scenario:

> A regional retailer's stores discard 14% more fresh food than last year. Forecast errors, late supplier substitutions, inconsistent ordering practices, and changing customer traffic have all been reported. The Chief Operations Officer proposes an AI demand-forecasting system that will automatically place orders for every store. The vendor reports 93% forecast accuracy and a potential 20% reduction in waste.

Ask the learner, one question at a time:

1. What are the symptoms, and what is not yet known?
2. State the problem without embedding a cause or solution.
3. Construct at least two causal branches.
4. What evidence would discriminate between them?
5. Apply the 4P framework.
6. Who owns the decision? Who is highly affected but may have little formal power?
7. Write the decision statement aloud.
8. Which assumptions should be tested first, and why?
9. Is AI appropriate? Compared with what?
10. What role should AI have initially?
11. Could a highly accurate forecast still produce a bad outcome?
12. What human judgment should dominate?
13. Define false-positive and false-negative equivalents in this context.
14. Design review, override, and stop triggers.
15. What data or result would change your recommendation?

### Part 3 — Adversarial follow-up

Select questions based on the learner's weaknesses:

- “The CEO already approved the vendor. Why keep the decision frame open?”
- “If humans make mistakes too, why should they retain authority?”
- “The model is more accurate on average. Why investigate subgroup or store-level results?”
- “Waste fell during the pilot. What else could explain it?”
- “Managers override only 1% of recommendations. Is that evidence of quality?”
- “A store employee has little influence. Why involve them?”
- “What assumption are you most reluctant to test?”
- “What would make you stop the system even if financial results improved?”

### Part 4 — Revision

Give one new fact that contradicts the learner's position. For example:

> Most waste growth is concentrated in stores affected by a supplier packaging change, and the simple seasonal rule performs nearly as well as the AI model.

Require the learner to revise the problem frame, assumption log, and AI suitability conclusion. The ability to update is part of mastery.

## Examination rubric

Score each dimension from 1 to 4.

| Dimension | 1 — Memorized or unsafe | 2 — Partial | 3 — Competent | 4 — Mastery |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Problem and cause reasoning | Jumps from symptom to preferred solution | Identifies some causes but weak evidence | Builds testable causal branches | Actively seeks disconfirmation and revises the frame |
| Decision framing | No clear owner or alternatives | Partially actionable statement | Clear owner, choice, objective, timing, evidence, and constraints | Neutral frame remains robust under challenge |
| Assumptions and evidence | Treats assumptions as facts | Lists assumptions | Prioritizes and validates material assumptions | Identifies evidence that would reverse the conclusion |
| Stakeholders and accountability | Focuses only on senior leaders | Includes affected parties | Maps authority, knowledge, action, challenge, and impact | Addresses power asymmetry and meaningful participation |
| AI suitability | Assumes AI is necessary or rejects it reflexively | Discusses benefits and generic risks | Compares alternatives and selects a bounded role | Integrates decision mechanism, data, error economics, and context |
| Human judgment and oversight | Adds a symbolic human reviewer | Names review without operational detail | Defines authority, information, time, override, escalation, and stop | Anticipates automation bias, incentives, and oversight failure |
| Adaptability | Defends original answer despite new facts | Makes cosmetic changes | Revises conclusion and artifacts appropriately | Explains exactly which evidence changed which assumption and why |

## Pass standard

The learner passes only when all of the following are true:

- Score of at least **3 in every dimension**
- No unresolved critical error involving accountability, safety, affected stakeholders, or unsupported causal claims
- Can explain the reasoning without reading definitions
- Can revise the recommendation when assumptions or evidence change
- Produces internally consistent versions of all five artifacts

A strong average cannot compensate for a score below 3 in a critical dimension.

## If the learner does not pass

1. Identify the exact reasoning failure.
2. Assign a smaller practice case focused on that failure.
3. Require the relevant artifact to be revised.
4. Re-examine with a different unseen case.
5. Continue to Phase 2 only after the pass standard is met.

# 8. Phase completion checklist

Before Phase 1 is complete, confirm that the learner can answer:

- What decision are we actually making?
- What is the evidence-supported problem rather than merely the symptom?
- Which root causes are supported, and which remain hypotheses?
- What assumptions could invalidate the recommendation?
- Who owns the decision and who bears its consequences?
- Is AI better than feasible simpler alternatives?
- What role should AI play, if any?
- When should human judgment dominate?
- What happens if AI is wrong?
- How will humans review, challenge, override, escalate, or stop?
- What evidence would make us change our mind?

Phase 1 ends only when the learner can defend these answers under oral challenge and update them when new evidence appears.

# 9. Delivery, access, and assessment controls

## Entry and workload

No technical build experience is required. Learners should be able to describe a real or synthetic organizational decision and perform basic percentage/rate interpretation. Use an entry diagnostic to identify support needs; do not infer readiness from job title.

Plan six facilitated sessions of **75–120 minutes** (7.5–12 contact hours) plus approximately **8–15 hours** of independent case and artifact work. These are planning ranges to be measured during pilot delivery, not evidence of actual learner workload.

## Common competency interpretation

The existing 1–4 artifact and examination rubrics map directly to the program levels: 1 Insufficient, 2 Developing, 3 Proficient, and 4 Advanced. Phase progression requires at least 3 in every assessed dimension and no unresolved critical error.

For summative decisions, the evaluator must cite artifact and oral evidence. A second calibrated evaluator reviews every hard-stop failure, appeal, and a defined sample of passing work. Resolve disagreements against the descriptors and evidence, not seniority or preferred conclusion.

## Accessible and equivalent participation

- Provide text equivalents, logical reading order, meaningful descriptions of visuals, and accessible tables for every case and presentation.
- Permit equivalent written, spoken, or assistive-technology-supported responses where speed or presentation style is not the construct.
- Supply a synthetic case when workplace information cannot be used safely.
- Never require confidential, personal, regulated, or security-sensitive information in an unapproved AI tool.
- Record approved accommodations and confirm that they preserve the same reasoning and transfer standard.

## Source and case control

Before each cohort, verify the current status of consequential governance and legal sources, freeze the learner and facilitator case versions, and keep hidden evidence separate. Worked answers are released only after the independent attempt is recorded.
